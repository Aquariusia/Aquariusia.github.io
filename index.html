Bachelor's thesis · The University of Sydney

Replicating and Critiquing Card (1993): Re-estimating the Returns to Education

The thesis revisits a classic question — what is a college education worth? — using a new dataset and updated instrumental-variable methods.Card's original idea: growing up near a college nudges people into more schooling for reasons unrelated to their ability, which makes distance a natural experiment.

Two results stand out. First, distance to college appears to weaken as an instrument once internet access and remote learning are widespread. Second, the marginal returns to education are substantially higher for low-income and rural groups, which supports targeted higher-education subsidies.

Read the abstract

Based on my bachelor's thesis, this study revisits David Card's (1993) estimate of the causal effect of college attendance on the returns to education, using a new dataset and several updated estimation methods. I re-examine the relationship between students' family proximity to a university and their educational outcomes, and ask whether distance remains a valid instrument once internet access and remote learning are widespread. I find evidence that the instrument's validity has weakened, consistent with structural behavioural shifts under digital transformation — adding a spatiotemporal perspective to one of econometrics' most widely used identification strategies. Heterogeneous-effects analyses further show that marginal returns to education are substantially higher for low-income and rural populations than for high-income and urban groups, providing quantitative evidence that targeted higher-education subsidies can deliver disproportionately large gains for disadvantaged communities and offering concrete guidance for equity-oriented policy design.
